Every wildfire large enough to enter the federal record across 11 Western states, 1984–2024, played one year at a time. Older burns fade but never vanish, so what builds up is the cumulative footprint of four decades of fire.

Drag the handle on the right to move through time. Click any fire for its name, date and size. Tapping the handle cycles the view: that season on its own, then where fires have overlapped, then back to the accumulated map. In that third view the handle drives repeat count rather than year — see below.

Most of what's mapped is unplanned wildfire, shown in the warm orange–red ramp. About 2% of the burned area, though, is prescribed fire — deliberately set for fuel reduction or habitat management — shown in blue instead, on the same fades-with-age ramp.

The bar under the title is that season's total acres burned, on a common scale — longest is 2020 at 9.2 million acres, shortest 1991 at 0.5 million.

Repeat burns

The third view asks a different question: where has the same ground burned more than once? Every mapped perimeter is stamped onto a 90 m grid, so each cell knows how many fires have crossed it. The whole 41-year footprint drops to a dim ember and the reburns are lit on top of it, hotter the more often they have burned.

Here the handle stops being a year and becomes a threshold — 2 at the bottom, up to 9 at the top. Drag it up and the ground that has only burned twice falls back into the ember, until what is left is the small amount that keeps burning. Click any patch for its own count.

About 19 million acres — a fifth of everything that has burned in the West since 1984 — has burned at least twice. Three million of those have burned three times, and 900,000 acres four times or more. At the top of the rail sits a single 144-acre hillside in the Santa Lucia Range behind the Big Sur coast: nine fires between 1992 and 2016.

The last two or three stops describe so little ground that it is smaller than a pixel on a map of the whole West — the acreage beside the title is the honest measure of them. Zoom in and they are there.

Fire data

MTBS — Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity, a joint USDA Forest Service and US Geological Survey program mapping every large fire since 1984 by the same method throughout.

Only fires of roughly 1,000 acres or more are mapped in the West, so small fires are genuinely absent rather than missing — about 95% of total burned area is still captured. The most recent season stays provisional for about a year while burn severity is reassessed, so 2024 may yet grow.
