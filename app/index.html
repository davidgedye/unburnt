Every wildfire large enough to enter the federal record across 11 Western states, 1984–2024, played one year at a time. Older burns fade but never vanish, so what builds up is the cumulative footprint of four decades of fire.

Drag the handle on the right to move through time, and click any fire for its name, date and size. The three buttons under the title switch what the map is showing: Single Year shows a season on its own, Accumulation builds the record up one season at a time, and Repetition leaves the timeline for the question below. The handle only moves through time; the buttons are what change the view.

Most of what's mapped is unplanned wildfire, shown in the warm orange–red ramp. About 2% of the burned area, though, is prescribed fire — deliberately set for fuel reduction or habitat management — shown in purple instead, on the same fades-with-age ramp.

The bar under the title is that season's total acres burned, on a common scale — longest is 2020 at 9.2 million acres, shortest 1991 at 0.5 million.

Repeat burns

The third view asks a different question: where has the same ground burned more than once? Every mapped perimeter is stamped onto a 90 m grid, so each cell knows how many fires have crossed it. The whole 41-year footprint drops to a dim ember and the reburns are lit on top of it, hotter the more often they have burned.

Here the handle stops being a year and becomes a threshold — 2 at the bottom, up to 9 at the top. Drag it up and the ground that has only burned twice falls back into the ember, until what is left is the small amount that keeps burning. Click any patch for its own count.

About 19 million acres — a fifth of everything that has burned in the West since 1984 — has burned at least twice. Three million of those have burned three times, and 900,000 acres four times or more. At the top of the rail sits a single 144-acre hillside in the Santa Lucia Range behind the Big Sur coast: nine fires between 1992 and 2016.

The last two or three stops describe so little ground that it is smaller than a pixel on a map of the whole West — the acreage beside the title is the honest measure of them. Zoom in and they are there.

How hard it burned

A perimeter says a fire reached here; it doesn't say what it did. Single Year repaints every fire MTBS has mapped by severity at once: amber through orange to deep red where the canopy went, against the plain orange of the fires it has not reached yet. In Accumulation that would be four decades of it at once, so there a click asks for one fire. Ground that barely burned is left unpainted, so it reads as holes in the burn rather than as a colour of its own, and the popup lists only what you can see.

Severity is 30 m detail about a single fire, so it is offered where it can actually be read — one fire, close up — rather than as a layer over the whole West, and each fire's data is only fetched when you ask for it.

Not every fire has it. MTBS assesses severity a season or two behind the perimeters, so recent fires often have none yet, and a fire that says severity not yet mapped genuinely has none rather than having failed to load. A few older fires say severity not currently available instead: those were assessed, but the published data for their year cannot presently be obtained. The percentages are shares of the area MTBS assessed, and do not add to a hundred: the barely-burned share is not among them.

Your own tracks

Drag a GPX file anywhere onto the map, or use + Add a GPX track under the title, and your route is drawn over the fire record in mint green. Several files at once is fine, and a file holding several tracks becomes one entry each. Click a track to see how long it is, how much of it has burned since 1984, and which fires did it — most recent first.

Nothing is uploaded. The file is read inside your browser and never leaves it: there is no account, no server, and nowhere for anyone else to see where you walk. Your tracks are remembered between visits by this browser alone, and the × beside a track forgets it for good.

The burn figure is measured by stepping along the route about every 40 m and asking which mapped perimeters contain each step, so it inherits the same two caveats as the repeat view: perimeters are simplified to roughly 200 m, and being inside one means a fire was mapped there, not that every acre of it burned.

Fire data

MTBS — Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity, a joint USDA Forest Service and US Geological Survey program mapping every large fire since 1984 by the same method throughout.

Only fires of roughly 1,000 acres or more are mapped in the West, so small fires are genuinely absent rather than missing — about 95% of total burned area is still captured. The most recent season stays provisional for about a year while burn severity is reassessed, so 2024 may yet grow.
